Abstract
Cyber threat intelligence (CTI) encoded in Structured Threat Information Expression (STIX) and structured according to the MITRE
ATT&CK framework has become a global reference for describing
adversary behavior. However, ATT&CK was designed as a descriptive knowledge base rather than a procedural model. We therefore
ask whether its structured artifacts contain sufficient behavioral
detail to support multi-stage adversary emulation. Through systematic measurements of the ATT&CK Enterprise bundle, we show
that campaign objects encode just fragmented slices of behavior.
Only 35.6% of techniques appear in at least one campaign, and
neither clustering nor sequence analysis reveals any reusable behavioral structure under technique overlap or Longest Common
Subsequence (LCS)-based analyses. Intrusion sets cover a broader
portion of the technique space, yet omit the procedural semantics
required to transform behavioral knowledge into executable chains,
including ordering, preconditions, and environmental assumptions.
These findings reveal a procedural semantic gap in current CTI
standards: they describe what adversaries do, but not exactly how
that behavior was operationalized.
To assess how far this gap can be bridged in practice, we introduce a three-stage methodology that translates behavioral information from structured CTI into executable steps and makes the
necessary environmental assumptions explicit. We demonstrate its
viability by instantiating the resulting steps as operations in the
MITRE Caldera framework. Case studies of ShadowRay and Soft
Cell show that structured CTI can enable the emulation of multistage APT campaigns, but only when analyst-supplied parameters
and assumptions are explicitly recorded. This, in turn, exposes

Introduction

Advanced Persistent Threats (APTs) unfold as multi-stage campaigns that may span multiple hosts, adapt to the target environment, and frequently rely on “living-off-the-land” techniques that
blend with legitimate activity [4]. Their distributed low-signal activities [41] and prolonged dwell times [28] make end-to-end reconstruction complex in Security Operations Centers (SOCs) [8, 15, 26].
Provenance-based detection and learning-driven approaches show
that reconstructing multi-step behaviors induces dependency explosion and yields large, multi-rooted provenance graphs [7, 18, 19, 39],
and that short or synthetic traces fail to represent characteristic
APT dependencies [17, 28, 41].
Cyber Threat Intelligence (CTI) provides structured accounts
of adversary tactics and techniques. Public feeds exhibit heterogeneous formats, uneven granularity, and inconsistent field usage [21, 38]. However, extracting actionable information from narrative reports requires substantial manual effort [32], and most CTI
sources lack the procedural context and environmental constraints
necessary for reproducible multi-host emulation [38]. Analyses of
intrusion-detection rules additionally show rapid turnover, skewed

ASIACCS ’26, June 1-5, 2026, Bangalore, India

alert distributions, and weak links between rules, alerts, and incidents [40]. As a result, CTI is used for mapping and coverage but
rarely as a direct source of executable adversary actions.
The MITRE ATT&CK framework provides a widely adopted
taxonomy of tactics and techniques, disseminated as STIX bundles
consumed by defensive platforms [29, 37, 42]. These bundles integrate elements such as intrusion sets, campaigns, malware, tools,
and relationships, but intrusion-set and campaign entries rarely
encode temporal or causal information. Execution order, prerequisites, and environmental assumptions typically appear only in
free text [29, 32, 38]. Prior work extracts partial behaviors from
textual intelligence without producing executable actions [6, 32, 45]
or reconstructs long-duration campaigns from telemetry [7, 39, 41],
leaving open the question of how far ATT&CK-in-STIX can support
APT emulation.
Frameworks such as MITRE Caldera provide an execution substrate for emulating adversary behavior, but they do not provide
procedural logic on their own. Capabilities must still be instantiated with an explicit sequence, parameters, and environmental
assumptions. These elements depend on a concrete System Under Test (SUT), whose operating systems, services, identity model,
and control stack determine which procedures are executable [35].
Current CTI standards do not encode SUT-level information or
execution constraints, and they cannot, on their own, automatically
generate full operations. In our case studies, analyst input is limited
to explicitly supplying such assumptions rather than reconstructing
the original campaigns; this is sufficient to produce reproducible
multi-step emulations. This gap motivates the central questions
guiding this study:
RQ1: How much adversarial behavior do current structured CTI
formats capture for multi-step APT emulation?
RQ2: How can structured CTI be transformed into computable
multi-stage behavioral plans for emulation?
RQ3: Which structural and semantic gaps in descriptive CTI prevent automation, and how can they be mitigated?
Our contributions are as follows: ① we quantify the coverage,
sparsity, and overlap of STIX objects in datasets derived from
MITRE ATT&CK; ② we examine inconsistencies and techniquelevel heterogeneity across campaigns and associated intrusion sets,
noting the absence of explicit behavioral structure for multi-stage
and multi-host emulation; ③ we identify structural gaps, including
the lack of sequencing, preconditions, and environmental assumptions, which limit the use of STIX data for automated execution;
and ④ we present two case studies and discuss implications for CTIbased APT emulation and data improvements. Taken together, these
results position our work as a boundary-mapping, measurementdriven study of what current CTI, as structured in STIX objects,
can and cannot support for automation, rather than solely as an
automation proposal. Using structured STIX intelligence to simulate adversary campaigns helps assess defensive controls, detection
methods, and overall cybersecurity strength in controlled environments.

Background

CTI refers to evidence-based knowledge about cyber threats that
supports analysts in decision-making, spanning from low-level
artifacts (IoCs) to descriptions of tactics, techniques, and procedures (TTPs). In its unstructured form, CTI appears as narrative reports, blog posts, and vendor documents, requiring manual effort to deduplicate, normalize, and extract information before it can be used systematically. To address this heterogeneity, the STIX language was developed by defining a standardized schema for objects and relationships to represent CTI in a
machine-readable format [29]. The STIX 2.1 specification, standardized by OASIS, defines 18 STIX Domain Objects (SDOs), covering both high-level entities (e.g., attack-pattern, campaign,
intrusion-set, threat-actor) and operational elements (e.g.,
malware, tool, infrastructure, indicator, observed-data,
report, vulnerability). Each SDO includes required and optional
fields. Relationships between objects are encoded using SDOs of
type relationship and grouping, which, for example, link campaigns to intrusion sets or techniques to malware. STIX is a generic
interchange format. Although the language originated in MITRE’s
efforts, it is now independently standardized and used by multiple CTI providers. Sources such as CAPEC, DigitalSide, AlienVault
Open Threat eXchange (OTX), and various TAXII servers described
in [21] publish collections of STIX objects, often with heterogeneous
schemas and reporting practices.
We use a subset of STIX 2.x objects extracted from multiple
CTI sources. The most relevant objects for modeling APTs are
attack-pattern, campaign, intrusion-set, malware, tool, and
relationship. These SDOs form the core of most mining, normalization, and knowledge graph construction efforts in CTI [6, 32, 38].
Objects such as indicator, observed-data, note, opinion, and
report complement this core with metadata, observational context,
and detection artifacts, but do not, on their own, form a campaign
procedure chain.
Beyond STIX, other structured models have also been proposed.
The MISP format, adopted by coalitions such as NATO (North Atlantic Treaty Organization) for its operational focus, offers a lighter,
community-driven alternative that prioritizes the sharing of events
and IoCs, with taxonomies and galaxies for categorization. The
CASE (Cyber-investigation Analysis Standard Expression) model,
based on the Unified Cyber Ontology (UCO), extends the scope to
unify CTI with digital forensic and investigative data, providing
greater semantic reasoning and traceability across domains. These
models illustrate a common trade-off: STIX provides high expressivity (the “what”, “who”, “why”, and, to some extent, the “how”) at
the cost of increased complexity; MISP emphasizes simplicity and
operational agility but offers less detail and flexibility; and CASE
focuses on semantic integration across evidence sources, which
may require greater modeling effort and standardization.
The use of STIX as a standardized CTI language mitigates the
limitations of proprietary or purely textual formats. Structured

representation enables automated ingestion by security platforms,
consistent mapping between entities (e.g., campaigns, threat actors,
malware families), and data correlation across organizations, supported by transport protocols such as TAXII (Trusted Automated
eXchange of Indicator Information). Widely used tools such as
Elasticsearch, Splunk, MISP, ELK-based stacks, IBM Resilient, and
CISA AIS already support STIX or provide connectors that convert proprietary formats into STIX. Libraries such as STIX-Shifter
treat STIX as a pivot representation, translating queries and data
between heterogeneous backends.
Despite this adoption, the availability of structured feeds remains
discontinuous. Longitudinal analyses show that several previously
open sources have been discontinued or migrated to commercial
models: Hail a TAXII, Unit42, and Anomali Limo no longer provide
open data; PickUpSTIX and Cyware have shifted content to commercial offerings; DigitalSide has not been updated for over a year;
and JamesBrine currently shares only IoCs [21]. There is a trend
toward fewer free STIX feeds, with a direct impact on studies that
rely on open, regularly maintained CTI.
MITRE maintains the ATT&CK framework as a hierarchical
knowledge base of TTPs observed in real campaigns, organized into
matrices for different domains (Enterprise, Mobile, and ICS) [37].
In this taxonomy, tactics capture the adversary’s objectives (the
“why”), while techniques and sub-techniques describe the mode of
operation (the “how”). In addition to the matrix of tactics and
techniques, ATT&CK indexes threat groups, campaigns, software
(malware and tools), telemetry sources, and mitigation recommendations [37]. Content from ATT&CK and other knowledge bases
maintained by MITRE (such as CAPEC) is usually disseminated in
STIX format; that is, MITRE publishes STIX bundles that instantiate the ATT&CK taxonomy using objects such as attack-pattern,
intrusion-set, malware, tool, and relationship.
Separating conceptual layers, we have: ATT&CK as the ontology
of adversary behavior; STIX as the serialization language adopted
to publish this ontology; and the specific bundle (e.g., the Enterprise
ATT&CK bundle) as one among several STIX repositories in the
CTI ecosystem. In ATT&CK, each technique or sub-technique is
represented by an attack-pattern object, with structured fields
for description, kill chain phases, external references, and tactic
mappings. These techniques, mapped directly to attack-pattern,
form the primary abstraction for structuring behavioral information extracted from reports and threat databases [38]. Extraction
pipelines treat these objects as building blocks, aligning narrative
texts with standardized technique identifiers [5, 32, 38], thereby
making them central to constructing technique-level traces and
mapping high-level descriptions to ATT&CK semantics.
Objects of type campaign represent specific operations attributed
to a threat actor. They generally encode time windows, objectives,
targeted sectors, and a subset of associated techniques, malware,
or tools. In practice, however, CTI feeds rarely enumerate all steps,
prerequisites, or action sequences. Threat reports frequently omit
steps, merge distinct phases, or present only fragments of the intrusion [32]; large-scale measurements report inconsistent field usage,
missing attributes, and heterogeneous reporting practices across
providers [21]. As a result, campaigns encoded in STIX tend to lack
the procedural specificity required for direct replay.

intrusion-set objects aggregate long-term persistent behaviors of a threat group over multiple campaigns, including commonly
used malware, techniques, infrastructure, and motivations [1]. In
open STIX collections, campaigns are linked to intrusion sets via
relationship objects that encode attribution, tool usage, or technique associations. This modeling reflects how CTI providers conceptualize APTs: intrusion sets capture the persistent adversary
profile, while campaigns represent concrete operational instances.
Studies on detection rules and ATT&CK annotations show that even
relatively stable actor or technique categories are inconsistently
labeled across vendors, which reduces interoperability [40].
Despite its broad adoption, the STIX data model was designed
to describe threat entities and relationships, rather than to serve as
a mechanism for the automated reconstruction of multi-host adversary behavior [29]. Although ATT&CK content, as represented in
STIX bundles, provides structured identifiers for techniques, tactics,
campaigns, and intrusion sets, the schema does not directly encode
temporal order, explicit prerequisites, detailed environmental constraints (such as specific OS configurations, network topology, minimum privileges, etc.), or intermediate goals and branching within
a campaign. This disconnect constitutes a procedural semantic gap
between descriptive CTI and machine-actionable representations,
which we quantify in our analysis.
Put differently, structured CTI encoded in STIX bundles provides
enumerations of threat entities but not the procedural dependencies
needed to assemble executable chains. Relationships are essentially
associative: for example, a relationship might indicate that an intrusion set “uses” a given malware or that a campaign is “attributed
to” a group, without specifying how to chain these entities into
an executable sequence. Concrete procedures remain in free-text
description fields or associated reports [32, 37, 38]. Thus, graphs
constructed from ATT&CK data serialized as STIX bundles capture
which techniques, tools, and campaigns are linked, but do not show
exactly how to replay a campaign’s chain.
Works such as CTI-Lense [21] have highlighted quality and consistency issues in open collections of structured CTI, including
low coverage, delayed sharing, and uneven field usage, even in
STIX feeds. Vermeer et al. [40] show that detection rules annotated with ATT&CK techniques exhibit inconsistent labeling across
vendors and high turnover, making these annotations difficult to
use as a stable basis for longitudinal analysis. Recent efforts focused on automatic behavioral extraction from structured CTI or
reports [5, 6, 32, 38, 45] can map parts of descriptions to ATT&CK
techniques, but still do not produce executable sequences with
preconditions, parameters, and control logic.
Even when specific commands or scripts are extracted from
reports and converted into executable artifacts, as discussed in
works on procedure reconstruction [12], there is a gap between
the original textual representation and the fidelity of the resulting
emulation. Research on detection systems based on the MITRE
ATT&CK framework [33, 42] and rules annotated with ATT&CK
primarily evaluates technique coverage and detection performance
in isolated steps, rather than the ability to derive attack chains from
structured CTI.
Recent measurement studies of structured CTI encoded in
STIX [21, 33, 42] focus on coverage and annotation quality, but

do not evaluate whether it contains the semantics required for automation. Even when emulation tools such as Caldera are used
to evaluate controls, a manual step remains to transform CTI descriptions (including STIX bundles and ATT&CK pages) into executable, ordered, and parameterized plans. The current MITRE
ATT&CK artifacts were designed to describe threat knowledge, not
to serve directly as a programming language for adversary campaigns. From an emulation perspective, this absence of procedural
structure is matched by the lack of any SUT representation. Assumptions about reachable services, directory layouts, privilege
boundaries, deployed controls, or identity models, when present,
appear only in free text and are not tied to a formal SUT abstraction.
Consequently, current CTI contains no computable link between
techniques and the environments in which they are expected to
execute [35].

Related Work

Recent advances in structured threat intelligence, technique extraction, and adversary emulation have expanded the range of available
methodologies. Prior work analyzes the structure and quality of CTI
feeds published in STIX, including aspects such as heterogeneity,
coverage, and field consistency, as well as their impact on commercial platforms and detection evaluations [21, 29, 33, 37, 38, 40, 42].
These studies quantify inconsistencies in field usage, rule annotations, and sharing practices, and show that both ATT&CKannotated rules and other STIX sources suffer from uneven coverage and vendor-specific practices. However, they do not assess
whether public bundles contain sufficient procedural information
to support automated multi-host emulation, nor do they systematically investigate sequencing, operational dependencies, or the
environmental context required for automated execution. As a result, existing work falls short of directly converting descriptive CTI
into executable multi-step representations, motivating the approach
developed in this paper.
Solutions for deriving adversary behavior from unstructured text,
such as Extractor [32], CTI HAL [10], and NLP-based extraction
pipelines [5, 6, 16, 45], as well as LLM-powered command and
script reasoning [12], advance the identification and organization
of techniques, entities, and relationships. These approaches include
supervised and weakly supervised extraction, Systematization of
Knowledge (SoK)-style evaluations of TTP extraction quality, and
LLM-based knowledge graph construction from CTI. However, they
do not directly produce executable sequences from the structure in
CTI bundles, and they evaluate extraction quality rather than the
completeness and sufficiency of the procedural structure encoded
in STIX itself. Temporal relationships and execution conditions are
inferred from narrative descriptions rather than assessed against the
actual encoding in STIX bundles, or evaluated to determine whether
those bundles alone provide the structure needed for replay.
In the field of detection and investigation, systems based on
telemetry and provenance graphs, including provenance-based
EDR and endpoint monitoring, use ATT&CK as an annotation
taxonomy but reconstruct attack structure from logs rather than
from CTI. Provenance-based detectors such as HOLMES, UNICORN, and ATLAS [1, 17, 28], and later systems such as ALchemist and NODLINK [24, 47], demonstrate that correlating

Roth et al.

whole-system audit data is effective for reconstructing APT campaigns. Recent work on provenance graph learning and scalable
detection, including Flash, Kairos, DistDet, PROGRAPHER, and
ProvG-Searcher [2, 7, 14, 39, 46], improves the efficiency and
accuracy of anomaly detection and search over large graphs.
Other approaches focus on tactic and technique recognition or
online segmentation, such as TREC, TAPAS, SLOT, and OCRAPT [3, 25, 31, 48], or address lateral movement detection in temporal network graphs [22, 23]. Complementary measurements highlight practical and industrial aspects of provenance-based EDR and
auditing, including operating costs, triage effort, and logging limitations [13, 20]. Together, these works provide detailed visibility
into campaign behavior and operational constraints. However, they
do not examine the use of STIX as a primary procedural source
for emulation: in these approaches, attack chains are reconstructed
from telemetry rather than derived from CTI object structures.
Adversary emulation and frameworks, such as Laccolith [30],
PentestGPT [11], PentestAgent [34], and language model-driven
proposals for offensive planning and cyber deception [35, 36], rely
on manually curated procedures, environment models, and execution logic that specify ordering, preconditions, and cleanup. Their
evaluations are conducted on fixed or parameterized SUTs, often
using standardized lab topologies or web application environments
as benchmarks, which shows that reproducibility depends on the
description of attacker behavior and the explicit specification of
the SUT. Structured CTI provides technique identifiers and highlevel behavioral context but requires an additional translation step
before it can be executed as a multi-stage procedure. Our work
asks whether such a model can be derived directly from the STIX
representation itself. We evaluate the extent to which current STIXencoded ATT&CK data contains (or lacks) the procedural semantics
required for automated emulation.
Frameworks that operationalize adversary behavior help clarify
what structured CTI does and does not provide. Planning-based systems such as AURORA [44] model actions through explicit preconditions and effects; the Effects Language (EL) formalism [9] demonstrates that executable coordination requires ordering and guarded
effects; and knowledge graph approaches such as KNOWHOW [27]
and MultiKG [43] enrich CTI with structure derived from telemetry or cross-source aggregation. These systems rely on procedural
elements (ordering, parameters, bindings, and environment assumptions) that are not encoded in the current MITRE ATT&CK Enterprise bundle. Our methodology is therefore complementary: it identifies the procedural information missing from this bundle. It shows
how descriptive CTI can be converted into representations suitable
for multi-host emulation, while explicitly characterizing what current standards cannot automate. Additionally, to our knowledge, no
prior work treats the ATT&CK Enterprise bundle itself as the object
of measurement to assess its suitability for automated multi-step
emulation.

Data Sources

The selected dataset is the Enterprise bundle of MITRE ATT&CK
in STIX format, which serves as the primary source of structured
CTI for this study due to its broad adoption in academia and industry, open availability, and absence of usage restrictions. MITRE
distributes ATT&CK datasets for the Enterprise, Mobile, and ICS
domains, all encoded in STIX; the additional CTI collections examined here also follow the STIX object model. ATT&CK is widely
regarded as a de facto standard for structuring CTI. It is relied

Description

upon by platforms such as Microsoft Sentinel, Microsoft Defender1 ,
Falcon2 , and government initiatives such as CISA3 .
We use this bundle (v18.1) as the authoritative source for all
quantitative analyses in this study [37]. Additional non-versioned
CTI sources were retrieved on 28 November 2025. Table 1 lists the
objects obtained from each provider. The dataset will be released
publicly after the completion of the double-blind review process.
Although several feeds describe themselves as TTP collections, STIX
does not define a structured procedure object; procedural behavior
must therefore be reconstructed from contextual relationships such
as uses, attributed-to, targets, delivers, and executes, or from narrative
descriptions, particularly those found in campaign objects.
To ensure reproducibility, we specify the object counts used in
our analysis. The Enterprise bundle contains 52 campaign SDOs,
but one (C0033) lacks any uses relationships and is excluded, leaving
51 campaigns. The bundle also includes 20,048 relationship objects,
which encode all links among campaigns, intrusion sets, malware,
tools, and techniques. Similarly, although the bundle contains 916
attack-pattern objects, only 835 correspond to techniques or
sub-techniques with behavioral content, which underlie our quantitative metrics. All quantitative analyses presented in this paper
(coverage, sparsity, overlap, clustering, campaign-group alignment)
are computed exclusively on the Enterprise bundle. However, the
broader structural pipeline was initially applied to the composed
dataset, and the exploratory findings led us to focus the quantitative
analysis on the Enterprise subset to ensure consistency and depth
of behavioral analysis.
We process STIX 2.x objects representing APT groups, their
campaigns, observed behaviors, infrastructure, and kill-chain elements. This dataset catalogs indicators, tools, techniques, and
sub-techniques, along with their associations with campaigns and
intrusion sets. While these objects provide structured entities and
links, they do not encode execution logic, ordering, or temporal
constraints; such information must be inferred or reconstructed.
Additionally, we performed a deduplication analysis using multiple
comparison criteria across STIX objects. Apart from expected duplication in the EclecticIQ source, no redundant entries were found.
The consolidated dataset contains 85,089 distinct objects.

To evaluate the methodology, we instantiated two campaigns inspired by structured CTI, ShadowRay and Soft Cell. Techniques were
translated into executable steps with clearly stated assumptions,
and the resulting steps were imported into Caldera and executed
in an isolated environment. Each campaign was executed ten times
under identical conditions, and all executions completed successfully and consistently. These results show that descriptive CTI can
support reproducible execution chains: single-host or multi-host,
depending on the campaign, when supplemented with analystdriven reconstruction of missing procedural elements. They also
highlight the procedural gaps that prevent full automation under
current CTI standards.

process for transforming descriptive structured CTI into a computable behavioral representation suitable for multi-host adversary
emulation, while clearly delineating the limits imposed by existing
CTI semantics.

6.3

Stage 3: Emulation Integration

Stage 3 converts the translated steps into an executable adversary
representation. Any emulation environment capable of expressing
actions, plans, and controlled execution can instantiate this stage.
Each step becomes a platform-aware action with optional preconditions and postconditions. Ordered sets of actions form a behavioral
plan that approximates a campaign or long-term threat operation,
enabling consistency checks, assumption validation, and telemetry
collection.
In this study, we instantiate Stage 3 using Caldera. Translated
steps are mapped to abilities, adversary profiles, and operations.
Abilities specify execution logic, expected output, and cleanup procedures; adversary profiles define the ordered set of abilities; and
operations orchestrate execution, timing, agent assignment, and
logging. Support scripts automate ability ingestion, profile construction, and repeated execution via the REST API. Once the humancurated steps are defined, all repetitive aspects of emulation are
automated.
In our experiments, Stage 1 is instantiated with the STIX 2.1
Enterprise bundle; Stage 2 applies our translation procedure using
ATT&CK entries and structured CTI as authoritative sources; and

Behavioral Analysis

Our analysis starts from two complementary views of the data.
The first is the composed STIX dataset, which aggregates multiple public CTI collections (including MITRE ATT&CK Enterprise,
CAPEC, DigitalSide, AlienVault OTX, and EclecticIQ). The second
is the MITRE ATT&CK Enterprise bundle itself, i.e., the official
knowledge base maintained by MITRE for the Enterprise domain,
serialized as STIX 2.x objects and periodically updated. It is important to distinguish the roles of these components. STIX is a
generic interchange format that defines object types and relationship structures for CTI. ATT&CK is a behavioral knowledge base
that organizes tactics, techniques, intrusion sets, campaigns, malware, and tools. The Enterprise bundle is one concrete STIX export
of this knowledge base. The composed dataset broadens this export
by incorporating additional STIX feeds that follow the same data
model.
We first executed the whole processing pipeline on the composed
dataset by parsing all bundles, deduplicating objects, constructing a
typed graph, deriving campaign-technique vectors, and computing
coverage, overlap, and clustering statistics across sources. The additional feeds increase breadth and redundancy by contributing more
objects, including indicators and malware families. However, their
campaign and intrusion-set descriptions are typically shorter and
less structured than those found in MITRE ATT&CK Enterprise. As
a result, while emulation scenarios can be synthesized from nonMITRE sources, the resulting procedures are often weakly specified
due to missing operational detail and environmental context.
Consistent with prior work on CTI quality, we confirm that,
beyond the well-documented issues of sparsity, incompleteness,
and inconsistency, most STIX-encoded artifacts lack the semantic
and procedural structure required for emulation based solely on
them. Campaign objects in particular contain limited procedural
information, do not fully document the incident, and rarely describe
the target environment or preconditions. These aspects must be
inferred from narrative descriptions rather than from machineactionable fields, which constrains the degree of automation that

ASIACCS ’26, June 1-5, 2026, Bangalore, India

can be achieved. For emulation purposes, both the composed dataset
and the Enterprise bundle suffer from the same structural limitation:
STIX does not define a dedicated procedure object with explicit
ordering, preconditions, and environment bindings, and none of
the sources in our corpus introduces such a construct as a custom
extension.
Despite this limitation, the ATT&CK Enterprise bundle stands
out by providing richer, more consistent behavioral descriptions
of intrusion sets and campaigns than the other feeds in the composed dataset. This motivated our decision to use Enterprise as
the basis for the detailed behavioral analysis and clustering that
follows. We begin by quantifying how ATT&CK Enterprise campaigns and intrusion sets use techniques. Each campaign in the
Enterprise bundle is represented as a binary vector over the set
of Enterprise techniques, indicating which techniques appear at
least once in that campaign. Intrusion sets are defined in the same
way, based on STIX relationship objects linking intrusion sets
to attack-pattern or technique identifiers. Analysis of the Enterprise data indicates that the number of documented threat groups
exceeds the number of publicly available campaigns. Each group is
linked to one or more sets of techniques covering phases such as
Initial Access, Persistence, Privilege Escalation, Credential Access,
Discovery, Lateral Movement, Command and Control, Collection,
Exfiltration, and Impact. Taken together, these techniques form the
group’s operational profile. Understanding this profile is essential
for connecting activity across campaigns, attributing incidents to
specific actors, and designing defensive measures that focus on
behavior rather than isolated indicators.
We then compare campaigns with their corresponding intrusion
sets to assess the extent to which the Enterprise bundle maintains
internal consistency. After determining that the most reliable way
to extract meaningful behavioral information from campaigns is to
treat each campaign as a set of techniques, we examined how the
use of these techniques in each campaign relates to those attributed
to the corresponding intrusion set. For this analysis, we rely on the
Enterprise STIX bundle to ensure that both sides of the comparison
(campaigns and groups) come from the same representation used
for coverage and clustering. We then manually cross-check a sample
of these relationships against the ATT&CK website to validate the
reference technique sets.
By comparing the techniques observed in each campaign with
those listed for the associated intrusion set, we identify discrepancies in both directions. Some campaigns are linked to groups
even though they exhibit limited overlap with the techniques documented for those groups. In other cases, campaign technique sets
include behaviors that are not present in the intrusion-set profile. A
representative example is the Juice Mix campaign, which is linked
to the OilRig intrusion set(s): only 64.3% of the techniques attributed to OilRig are observed in the Juice Mix campaign, as encoded
in the Enterprise bundle. Manual verification against the ATT&CK
website confirms that the website-level information is internally
consistent. At the same time, the corresponding STIX file, updated
shortly before our analysis, does not fully reflect those associations.
This illustrates that even frequently updated bundles can contain
inconsistencies, because relationships are curated as data rather
than derived automatically from a single authoritative model.

These differences are likely a consequence of the update workflow: ATT&CK web content and downloadable STIX bundle follow
separate publication pipelines. The website is updated first, and
the Enterprise bundle is regenerated afterwards. During this window, relationships visible on the website may not yet be present in
the latest STIX bundle. For emulation, this means that campaigngroup mappings in the bundle should not be treated as authoritative
without cross-checking.
The composed dataset from Section 5 expands the set of STIX
objects and broadens coverage across threat domains, including
malware, infrastructure, and indicators [21]. Additional feeds increase diversity and allow cross-checks between providers. They do
not, however, change the fundamental structural properties of the
data: all sources rely on the same STIX object types, and none introduces structured fields for procedure, ordering, or environment
constraints beyond what the standard already provides.
In other words, the composed dataset improves breadth and redundancy, but not procedural depth. Campaigns and intrusion sets
from non-MITRE sources often have shorter, less detailed descriptions and fewer contextual hints about platform, prerequisites, or
execution details. It is still possible to derive emulation scenarios
from these sources, but the resulting procedures are necessarily
more approximate.
This explains why our quantitative behavioral analysis and emulation methodology is implemented on the ATT&CK Enterprise
bundle: it offers the most coherent and detailed combination of campaigns, intrusion sets, and techniques in our corpus, even though
it is not free of sparsity or inconsistency.

All executions completed without internal errors, indicating that
the translated technique sequences are procedurally coherent under fixed conditions. Average runtimes were approximately one
minute for ShadowRay and two minutes for Soft Cell. These results
confirm only that the CTI-derived steps, once supplemented with
analyst-driven procedural reconstruction of missing preconditions,
parameters, and environment assumptions, form internally consistent executable chains; they do not imply fidelity to the original
intrusions or that this reconstruction effort is negligible.
The emulation experiments also highlight some of the identified
structural limitations. All prerequisites and environmental dependencies must be manually introduced during translation. Branching
or conditional logic cannot be directly recovered from the structured representation. That is, the current ATT&CK Enterprise bundle, serialized in STIX, can support reproducible multi-step execution when supplemented with procedural details provided by
human analysts. Thus, the emulation experiments serve a dual role:
they demonstrate that descriptive CTI contains sufficient semantic
grounding to support procedural reconstruction when minimally
enriched, and they precisely reveal where current CTI formats lack
the structure required for automated adversary emulation.

Case Studies

From a universe of 51 campaigns in the MITRE ATT&CK Enterprise dataset, 187 intrusion-sets, 168 of them (89.8%) have at least
one associated technique, while 19 (10.2%) contain no technique
relationships. Across the populated intrusion sets, we observe 4,362
technique references, yielding an average of 26.0 techniques per
intrusion set. All intrusion sets that include at least one technique
are included in the technique-based measurements. On the technique side, we identified 30 techniques that are not translatable
into executable commands because they are platform-agnostic. As
a result, we were able to run 225 Caldera operations on a generic
SUT.
To ground our methodology, we selected one campaign that we
instantiated in a single-host emulation environment (ShadowRay)
and one campaign whose original intrusion spans multiple hosts
(Soft Cell) to determine how much of their documented behavior can
be operationalized from structured CTI and where analyst-supplied
assumptions remain unavoidable. The goal was not to reproduce
the historical intrusions, but to assess whether the technique-level
behavioral structure encoded in STIX and ATT&CK is sufficient to
instantiate coherent multi-step executions in an emulation environment.
ShadowRay is associated with exploiting perimeter systems and
abusing credentials, and it contains sufficient technique-level detail
to outline a behavioral chain. Soft Cell, a multi-year intrusion into
telecommunications networks, combines web exploitation, webshell persistence, Privilege Escalation, Credential Access, internal
reconnaissance, Lateral Movement, and staged data Exfiltration.
Despite differences in scope and tooling, both campaigns exhibit
similar structural gaps.
For each campaign, we extracted all relevant techniques from
their STIX objects and aligned them with the canonical tactic ordering used throughout this work. Following the methodology of

Section 6, each technique was translated into an executable step. Discovery and enumeration actions required only generic commands,
whereas steps related to Initial Access, Persistence, Privilege Escalation, and Exfiltration required parameters and assumptions not
present in CTI (e.g., file paths, hostnames, credentials, and privilege
levels).
Table 2 summarizes the alignment between the translated behaviors and their corresponding ATT&CK tactics. The tactic-level
overlap reflects standard APT tradecraft, while differences in associated techniques illustrate why campaign-level emulation cannot
be derived solely from templates. Each translated step was implemented as a single Caldera ability, using explicit commands
and cleanup logic. Both campaigns were executed ten times in
the isolated two-VM testbed described in Section 8. All executions
completed successfully and produced consistent outputs because,
during SUT deployment, we ensured this. Because Caldera performs
no inference or auto-correction, any missing parameter, privilege
assumption, or ordering dependency would have resulted in immediate failure, making these experiments a direct test of procedural
completeness.
The results make explicit the structural limitations identified in
Section 7. STIX objects do not encode execution order; they lack
parameters, environment bindings, and privilege requirements; and
they contain no branching logic or preconditions. Nevertheless, the
case studies show that structured CTI provides sufficient behavioral grounding to support reproducible multi-host emulation when
supplemented with a human-in-the-loop translation layer. This confirms the methodology’s viability and clarifies where current CTI
standards fall short for automation.

Discussion and Implications

Our results expose a structural gap at the core of current structured
CTI practice. STIX and ATT&CK provide a taxonomy of adversary
behavior, but they do not encode the procedural semantics required
for automation, such as ordering, preconditions, parameterization,
or environment bindings. This gap is distinct from previously studied limitations in CTI sharing [21], rule annotation variability [40],
or EDR evaluation [33]. Our quantitative analysis shows that campaigns exhibit sparse and heterogeneous use of techniques, lack
a stable behavioral backbone, and do not form meaningful clusters. The case studies further demonstrate that campaign objects
omit crucial operational details, forbade any attempt to reproduce
a campaign exactly how it happens. Each executable step requires
assumptions about the target environment, platform constraints,
or privilege levels that cannot be derived solely from structured
CTI. At the same time, the experiments show that once a human
analyst explicitly fills these gaps through non-trivial but bounded
procedural reconstruction, the resulting chains are coherent and
reproducible. Together, these findings clarify both the potential and
the limits of current structured CTI for multi-step emulation. These
results should be interpreted as measures of the semantic structure
encoded in STIX, rather than as attempts to reconstruct historical
campaign timelines or ground-truth procedures.
The case studies reveal where structured CTI is strong and where
it is silent. Technique associations provide behavioral grounding,
but the steps required for execution, including parameters, privilege

A second implication concerns environmental sensitivity. Real
intrusions reflect the victim organization’s topology, identity model,
security controls, and operational workflows. As shown in prior
work on provenance-based detection [17, 28, 39], behavior is profoundly shaped by environmental constraints. Because STIX does
not encode such context, emulations generated solely from CTI risk
misrepresenting preconditions or producing unrealistic execution
paths unless additional environment modeling is performed. This
requirement is inherent to the current CTI standards, which do not
encode SUT-level assumptions.
These characteristics have practical consequences for defenders.
CTI-derived emulations, once enriched, provide testable approximations of adversary workflows that can be used to expose visibility
gaps, validate telemetry and detection pipelines, and measure the
resilience of analytic rules. Variation in parameters, supporting
tools, or execution timing, whether manual or LLM-generated, introduces realistic diversity that reduces overfitting to fixed patterns
and supports the evaluation of detection robustness. This is particularly important because structured CTI alone does not provide
the granularity required to express realistic operational diversity.
The gap between descriptive CTI and operational automation
suggests an agenda. First, enriching STIX with optional procedural
fields would enable the community to share what adversaries do
and also the structure needed to reproduce it. Such extensions
need not modify the core STIX ontology; optional layers or companion schemas would suffice. Second, LLMs offer a promising
intermediate layer: they can consolidate heterogeneous reports,

Threats to Validity

Threats to construct validity arise because our measurements
reflect the behavioral structure explicitly encoded in STIX objects rather than complete ground-truth campaign procedures.
Campaign-technique vectors, sparsity, overlap, and clustering capture only what is documented in structured CTI, rather than complete execution sequences, causal dependencies, or operational
context. Likewise, the tactic-ordered lists used in our methodology
provide an organizational scaffold and are not intended to reconstruct actual temporal order. As a result, aspects such as failed

The Procedural Semantics Gap in Structured CTI: A Measurement-Driven STIX Analysis for APT Emulation

attempts, branching logic, concurrency, and environment-specific
constraints are outside the scope of our analysis.
Threats to internal validity stem primarily from data quality and
curation practices. The downloadable ATT&CK Enterprise STIX
bundle may lag behind website updates due to asynchronous publication pipelines, leading to incomplete or outdated campaign-group
relationships. In addition, public CTI exhibits curation bias: only a
subset of known operations is represented in structured form, and
documentation quality varies across campaigns and intrusion sets.
We mitigate these risks through deterministic parsing, consistent
normalization of objects and relationships, validation of counts and
links, and manual cross-checks against the authoritative ATT&CK
website. During emulation, all experiments are conducted in an
isolated testbed, using clean snapshot restoration and repeated
runs to ensure that observed failures reflect missing procedural
information in CTI rather than implementation artifacts.
External validity is limited by both the scope of available CTI
and the degree of abstraction in the emulation environment. Our
quantitative analysis focuses on the ATT&CK Enterprise dataset
and does not cover proprietary sources or other domains such as
Mobile or ICS. The emulation environment is intentionally simplified and designed to test procedural coherence, not to reproduce
historical victim infrastructures. Because STIX does not encode
preconditions, parameters, privilege requirements, or environment
bindings, the translation step necessarily involves explicit human
assumptions. Different analysts may therefore derive slightly different, yet behaviorally consistent, executions. These limitations
reflect structural properties of current CTI standards and bound the
degree of automation achievable with publicly available structured
intelligence.

12

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that current structured CTI, as represented
in publicly available STIX, provides only fragmented and partial
coverage of adversarial behavior, making it insufficient for direct
multi-stage APT emulation (RQ1). Both campaign entries and intrusion sets lack essential procedural elements, such as step ordering,
preconditions, parameterization, and environment assumptions,
thereby precluding executable automation. To address these limitations, we proposed a three-stage methodology that combines
tactic-ordered behavioral scaffolds with analyst-driven procedural
reconstruction to transform descriptive CTI into computable behavioral plans (RQ2). Our case studies showed that reproducible
multi-stage execution relies on this supplementation, clarifying
the operational boundaries of current standards. These results underscore that campaign-centric emulation cannot rely on generic
templates, as public CTI does not encode reusable procedural chains.
The main barriers to automation, namely the absence of ordering,
preconditions, and environment context, can be mitigated by extending CTI schemas with optional procedural fields and incorporating translation layers (RQ3). We delineate the boundary between
descriptive CTI and the procedural representations needed to support automated multi-step execution.
